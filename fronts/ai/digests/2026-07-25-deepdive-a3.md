Two episodes ago I called the KV cache the hidden protagonist of inference, and last episode, when we took attention apart, I kept pointing at it — it's what grouped-query attention exists to shrink, it's the reason keys and values get saved while queries get thrown away. I've been circling it on purpose. Now we land on it, because once you understand this one data structure, you understand the economics of the entire serving industry: why context windows have the limits they have, why long conversations get more expensive as they go, why prompt caching gives you a ninety percent discount, and why the person running the GPU cares more about memory than about compute. By the end of this episode the cache will stop feeling like an optimization and start feeling like what it actually is — the load-bearing wall the whole serving stack is built around.

Let's start by nailing down exactly what it is, because you now have every piece.

Recall the loop from the inference episode. To generate text, the model runs one full forward pass per token, and it's serial — token five hundred can't be computed until token four hundred ninety-nine exists. And recall from last episode what happens inside each of those passes: attention, where every token computes a query and dots it against the key of every earlier token, then blends their values. Put those two facts together and you see the disaster waiting to happen. To generate token five hundred and one, attention needs the keys and values of all five hundred prior tokens. If you recomputed them from scratch every step, you'd redo the entire history on every single token — five hundred tokens' worth of work to produce one. The cost would be quadratic in the length of what you've generated, and generation would grind to a halt.

The KV cache is the fix, and it's almost embarrassingly simple as an idea. When you process a token, you compute its key vector and its value vector once — and then you keep them. You stash them in GPU memory. On the next step, the new token computes its query, dots it against every cached key, blends every cached value, and then adds its own freshly computed key and value to the pile for next time. That's the whole trick. Each decode step does the attention work of exactly one new token against a growing cache, instead of redoing all of history. It converts a quadratic catastrophe into something linear. The query, notice, is never cached — it's computed, used for one step, discarded. Only keys and values persist, because only they are things that future tokens will need to look back at. That asymmetry, which sounded like trivia last episode, is the entire reason the structure is called the KV cache and not something else.

So the cache is what makes decode affordable. But nothing is free, and what you bought speed with was memory. Let's put a number on exactly how much, because this is the number that governs everything downstream.

Here's the sizing, in words. For every token you keep in context, you store a key vector and a value vector — that's the factor of two — and you store them not once but for every layer of the model, because each layer does its own attention with its own keys and values. And within each layer you store them for every key-value head. So the memory for one token is: two, for keys and values, times the number of layers, times the number of key-value heads, times the size of each head, times the bytes per number. Let's make it concrete with a modern seventy-billion-parameter model in the grouped-query configuration we discussed last time — eighty layers, eight key-value heads, each head a hundred and twenty-eight dimensions wide, two bytes per number at sixteen-bit precision. Multiply it through and you get roughly a third of a megabyte per token. Call it three hundred kilobytes.

That sounds tiny. It is not tiny, and here's where it bites. A third of a megabyte per token, times a context of a hundred and twenty-eight thousand tokens, is around forty gigabytes. Forty gigabytes of memory — for one request. To put that in scale, the model's own weights at this size are about a hundred and forty gigabytes. So a single long-context request can demand a KV cache that's a meaningful fraction of the entire model. And you don't serve one request; you serve many at once, because — remember from the inference episode — decode is memory-bandwidth-bound, so you batch requests together to amortize the cost of streaming the weights. Every request in that batch brings its own KV cache. Now the arithmetic turns hostile fast: the model weights are a fixed cost you pay once and share across the whole batch, but the KV cache is a per-request cost that scales with how many users you're serving times how long each of their contexts is. On a data-center GPU with, say, eighty gigabytes of memory, the weights eat a hundred and forty — already you need two GPUs just to hold the model — and whatever memory is left over after the weights is the entire budget for every concurrent user's cache. That leftover memory, divided by the cache-per-request, is a hard ceiling on how many people you can serve at once. The KV cache, not the compute, is what caps your batch size. And your batch size is what determines your throughput and therefore your cost per token. Follow that chain and you arrive at the industry's actual bottom line: for most serving, the binding constraint is KV cache capacity.

Now pause on the last episode's payoff, because it lands with full weight here. I said grouped-query attention shrinks the cache. Look at what that means now with the number in front of you. Old-style multi-head attention with sixty-four key-value heads instead of eight would multiply that third of a megabyte by eight — two and a half megabytes per token, forty gigabytes becomes over three hundred. The eight-times cache reduction from grouped-query attention isn't a nice-to-have; it's the difference between fitting a handful of long-context users on a GPU and fitting almost none. That's why grouped-query went from a research idea to the default in every serious open model in about two years. When you understand the cache, the model card stops being a list of arbitrary choices and becomes a series of moves in a memory budget.

Let's connect this to the two phases from the inference episode, because the cache behaves completely differently in each, and the difference is where prompt caching comes from.

Prefill — processing your prompt — is where the cache gets written. Your prompt tokens all exist at once, so the model processes them in parallel, one big efficient pass, and as it does, it computes and stores the key and value for every prompt token at every layer. At the end of prefill, the cache is fully populated for your entire prompt, and you've paid the compute to build it. Decode — generating the response — is where the cache gets read and extended. Each new token reads the whole existing cache to do its attention, then appends its own one token's worth. So prefill is a burst of parallel compute that fills the cache; decode is a long serial crawl that reads it and grows it by one each step.

Here is the thing that observation unlocks. The keys and values for a given prompt, at a given position, depend only on the tokens up to that position. They are deterministic. Which means: if two requests share a prefix — say, the same long system prompt — the KV cache for that prefix is identical. Compute it once, and you never have to compute it again for any request that starts the same way. That is exactly what prompt caching is. When a provider offers you a ninety percent discount on cached input tokens, they are telling you that the expensive part of prefill — building the KV cache for that shared prefix — was already done and sitting in memory from a previous request, so they skipped it and passed you most of the saving. You are looking directly at this data structure on your invoice. And it tells you how to write prompts that are cheap: put the stable, reusable material — the long system prompt, the fixed instructions, the few-shot examples — at the front, unchanged, so its cache can be reused, and put the variable per-request material at the end. Reorder your prompt and you can move the cache boundary and change what you pay. Most engineers never realize they have that lever.

So the cache explains your bill. Now let's go where last episode promised we'd go — inside the memory, to see why managing this thing naively wastes most of your GPU, and what fixed it.

Think about the awkward shape of the problem the serving system faces. A request arrives, and you have no idea how long it will get. The user might generate ten tokens or ten thousand. But the cache grows one token at a time, unpredictably, and it has to live in contiguous-feeling memory so attention can sweep across it efficiently. The obvious, naive thing to do is reserve the maximum up front: when a request comes in, allocate a slab of memory big enough for the model's entire context window, just in case it runs long. And this is a catastrophe, in three distinct ways that are worth separating because they have different names and you'll hear them.

The first is reserved waste. If you allocate for thirty-two thousand tokens because that's the max, but the request only ever uses five hundred, the other thirty-one and a half thousand tokens of memory sit reserved, untouchable by anyone else, for the entire life of that request. It's booked and empty. The second is internal fragmentation — even within what a request will eventually use, at any given moment before it gets there, the not-yet-generated portion of its reserved slab is dead space. The third is external fragmentation: because different requests reserve different-sized slabs, the free memory between them gets carved into oddly sized holes, and you can end up unable to fit a new request even though the total free memory would be plenty, because no single hole is big enough. Add these up and the measured result, in the systems before this problem was solved, was brutal: real serving stacks were using as little as twenty percent of their KV cache memory for actual tokens. Sixty to eighty percent of the most precious resource on the GPU — the resource that caps your batch size and therefore your cost — was being wasted on reservations and fragmentation. You were paying for memory to sit empty.

The fix is one of those ideas that's obvious only in hindsight, and it was lifted wholesale from a fifty-year-old part of computer science: virtual memory and paging, the thing your laptop's operating system uses to manage RAM. The algorithm is called paged attention, and here's the move. Stop demanding that a request's cache live in one contiguous slab. Instead, chop memory into small fixed-size blocks — each holding the keys and values for, say, sixteen tokens — and hand them out one at a time, only as a request actually fills them. A request holds a list of block pointers, not a reserved region. When it needs a seventeenth token, it grabs one more block from a shared pool, wherever there's a free one; the blocks don't have to be next to each other in physical memory, exactly like pages of virtual memory don't. The attention kernel is taught to follow the pointer list, gathering across scattered blocks.

Look at what that kills. Reserved waste is gone — you never allocate for a length you haven't reached, so a request uses almost exactly the memory its actual tokens need. External fragmentation is gone — every block is the same size, so any free block fits any request, and there are no awkward holes. The only waste left is internal: the last, partially filled block of each request, at most fifteen unused token slots. The measured waste drops from sixty-plus percent to under four. And because you've reclaimed all that memory, you can fit far more requests in the batch at once — the systems that introduced this reported two-to-four-times the throughput at the same latency, purely from managing memory better. Not a faster chip, not a smaller model, not a cleverer kernel. Just refusing to waste the cache. That's how much was being left on the floor.

And paging bought a second thing, almost for free, that closes the loop back to prompt caching. Once cache lives in shareable blocks with pointers, two requests with the same prefix can literally point at the same physical blocks — one copy of the shared prefix's cache, referenced by many requests. The system only makes a private copy when a request diverges and needs to write, the same copy-on-write trick operating systems use when they fork a process. That's what makes prefix sharing and prompt caching efficient in practice, and it's also what makes something like generating several parallel completions of the same prompt cheap — they all share the prompt's cache blocks and only branch where they differ. The block abstraction turned the cache from a private slab into a shared, reference-counted resource, and a whole family of optimizations fell out of that one change.

Let me pull the altitude back up, because it's easy to get lost in blocks and lose the shape.

The KV cache is what makes generation affordable — it trades memory for the compute you'd otherwise burn recomputing history every step. But that trade puts memory, not arithmetic, at the center of the serving problem. The cache is per-request and grows with context length, so it, not the model weights and not the compute, is what caps how many users you can serve at once — which is what sets your cost. Everything I've described this episode is the industry fighting over that memory: grouped-query attention shrinks the cache at the source, paged attention stops you from wasting the memory you have, and prefix sharing lets many requests split one copy.

Three takeaways to carry out of here.

One. The KV cache converts decode from quadratic to linear by storing every token's keys and values instead of recomputing them, and the price is memory that scales with your context length times your batch size. When you hit a context-window limit, when a long conversation costs more per turn than a short one, when a provider can't give you a bigger batch discount — you're not hitting a compute wall, you're hitting the cache filling up memory. Memory is the currency of serving.

Two. Prefill fills the cache, decode reads and grows it, and because the cache for a given prefix is deterministic, an identical prefix's cache can be computed once and reused. That is the entire mechanism behind prompt caching. Put your stable material — system prompt, instructions, examples — first and unchanged, your variable material last, and you can turn most of your prefill into a cache hit. It's one of the few genuine free lunches you have as an application engineer, and it's a direct consequence of how this data structure works.

Three. Naive cache management wastes most of your GPU memory to reservation and fragmentation; paged attention borrowed operating-system paging to cut that waste from over sixty percent to under four, doubling to quadrupling throughput with no change to the model at all. When you choose a serving stack, you are largely choosing how well it manages this one data structure — which is why "which inference server" is a question worth taking as seriously as "which model."
