You've shipped systems on top of language models. You've handled their failures, built retry loops, designed human-in-the-loop checkpoints. But here's a question worth sitting with: when you send a request to a model API, what physically happens between your HTTP call and the first token coming back? If your honest answer is "a big matrix multiply, somehow," this episode is for you. By the end you'll be able to trace a request end to end, and — more usefully — you'll understand why the pricing page, the latency behavior, and the context window limits look the way they do.

Let's start where every request starts: with text becoming numbers.

Part one. Tokenization.

The model never sees your text. It sees a sequence of integers. The tokenizer is a lookup system, built before the model was ever trained, that chops text into pieces from a fixed vocabulary — typically somewhere between fifty thousand and two hundred and fifty thousand entries. The dominant algorithm is byte pair encoding. The idea is almost embarrassingly simple: start with raw bytes as your alphabet, scan a huge corpus, and repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair into a new vocabulary entry. Do that a hundred thousand times and you get a vocabulary where common words like "the" are one token, common fragments like "ization" are one token, and rare strings fall back to smaller pieces, all the way down to single bytes if needed.

Three practical consequences follow directly, and you've hit all three without necessarily knowing why. First, token counts are not word counts. English averages roughly three quarters of a word per token, but code, non-English languages, and unusual formatting can be dramatically less efficient. That's why the same semantic payload in Spanish costs more tokens than in English — the vocabulary was optimized for the training distribution, which skews English. Second, the classic failures — models struggling to count letters in a word, or to reverse a string — aren't reasoning failures exactly. The model literally cannot see characters; "strawberry" might be one or two tokens, and the letters inside are not part of its input. Third, the tokenizer is frozen. It's a separate artifact from the model weights, trained once, and every quirk of it is baked into everything downstream.

So now your prompt is a list of integers. Say, one thousand of them. What happens next?

Part two. Embeddings — from integers to geometry.

Each integer indexes into a giant lookup table called the embedding matrix. Each row of that table is a vector — for a mid-sized model, maybe four thousand ninety-six numbers. So token number twelve thousand becomes row twelve thousand: a point in a four-thousand-dimensional space. Your thousand-token prompt is now a thousand-by-four-thousand matrix of floating point numbers.

Here's the mental model that matters: everything the model does from now on is moving those points around in that space. The embedding table is learned during training, and it ends up organized so that geometry encodes meaning — directions in that space correspond to semantic relationships. But the raw embedding of a token is just its dictionary meaning, context-free. The word "bank" starts at the same point whether the sentence is about rivers or money. The entire job of the layers that follow is to move each token's vector to where it belongs given its context.

Part three. The forward pass — a stack of identical blocks.

A transformer is a stack of identical blocks — thirty-ish for a small model, eighty or more for a large one — and each block does exactly two things, in sequence.

Thing one: attention. This is the only place in the entire architecture where tokens interact with each other. Each token's vector gets to look at every token before it and pull in information. The "bank" vector looks back, notices "river" three tokens ago, and shifts itself toward the riverbank region of the space. We're doing a full episode on the mechanics — queries, keys, values, multiple heads — so for today, hold just this: attention is where context happens, and its cost grows with how many tokens are looking at how many other tokens. That growth is the deep reason long context is expensive, and we'll get to exactly how the cost lands in a moment.

Thing two: the feed-forward network, sometimes called the MLP block. After attention gathers context, each token's vector — independently now, no interaction — gets pushed through a two-layer neural network that expands it to maybe four times its width and squeezes it back. This is where most of the model's parameters live, roughly two thirds of them, and the best current understanding is that it acts as the model's knowledge store: a massive learned key-value memory where facts and patterns absorbed during training get applied. Attention decides what's relevant; the feed-forward block does something with it.

Then the block hands its output to the next identical block, and the process repeats. Early layers tend to resolve syntax and local structure; middle layers do the heavy semantic lifting; late layers converge on the actual prediction. By the final layer, the vector sitting at the last position of your sequence has absorbed, through attention, information from the entire prompt. That one vector is about to become your next token.

Part four. The logits, and where "randomness" actually lives.

That final vector gets multiplied by one last matrix — often just the embedding table reused in reverse — producing one score per vocabulary entry. Fifty thousand-plus raw numbers called logits. A softmax turns them into a probability distribution: the model's actual, complete output. Not a token — a distribution over every possible next token.

Everything after this point is not the model. It's a sampler: a few dozen lines of ordinary code that picks one token from that distribution. Temperature scales the logits before the softmax — low temperature sharpens the distribution toward the top choice, high temperature flattens it. Top-p truncates the long tail. Greedy decoding just takes the argmax. When people say the model is "creative" or "random," they're describing sampler settings, not the network — the forward pass itself is deterministic matrix arithmetic. There's a full episode coming on sampling, including the genuinely interesting question of why even temperature zero doesn't always give you bit-identical outputs in production.

So one forward pass produced one token. Now what? Now the brutal part.

Part five. Autoregression — the loop that explains your latency.

To get the second token, the model appends the first generated token to the sequence and runs again. And again. A five-hundred-token response is five hundred sequential forward passes through the entire stack of layers. Sequential is the key word — token two hundred cannot be computed before token one hundred ninety-nine exists. No amount of parallel hardware removes that dependency.

This is the single most important structural fact about LLM inference, and it splits every request into two phases with completely different characters.

Phase one is prefill: processing your prompt. All thousand prompt tokens can be processed in parallel — they all exist already, so the hardware chews through them in one big, efficient, compute-dense pass. Prefill is fast per token and it's what determines time to first token.

Phase two is decode: generating the response, one token per forward pass, serially. Each of those passes does a small amount of arithmetic but must stream tens of gigabytes of model weights from GPU memory to the compute units — for every single token. The arithmetic units spend most of their time waiting for memory. Decode is memory-bandwidth-bound, not compute-bound, and that asymmetry is worth tattooing somewhere: prefill is compute-bound and parallel, decode is bandwidth-bound and serial.

Look at any provider's pricing page with this in mind and it snaps into focus. Output tokens cost several times more than input tokens — not marketing, just physics: input tokens are processed in one parallel pass, output tokens each require a full serial trip through the machine. Time to first token scales with prompt length, because that's prefill. Tokens per second after the first is roughly constant, because that's the decode treadmill.

One more piece and the picture is complete.

Part six. The KV cache — the memory that makes decode possible at all.

Naively, generating token five hundred and one means re-running attention over all five hundred previous tokens from scratch — redoing work you already did. Every serious inference stack avoids this by caching the intermediate attention states — the keys and values — for every token already processed. That's the KV cache. With it, each decode step only computes attention state for the one new token and reads the rest from cache.

The catch: that cache is enormous. It grows linearly with sequence length, and for long contexts it can rival the size of the model weights themselves — gigabytes of GPU memory per single request. This is why context windows have hard limits, why long conversations cost more, and why providers offer prompt caching discounts: if your system prompt's KV states are already sitting in memory from a previous request, prefill for that portion is nearly free, and they pass some of that saving to you. When you see a ninety percent discount on cached input tokens, you're looking directly at this mechanism. The KV cache gets its own full episode, because it is quietly the central resource constraint of the entire serving industry.

Part seven. What a token costs — putting the whole picture together.

Let's do the back-of-envelope, because it's the kind you can now do from first principles. A rule of thumb: generating one token requires roughly two floating point operations per model parameter. A seventy-billion-parameter model: about a hundred and forty billion operations per token. A modern data center GPU does on the order of a quadrillion operations per second, so the arithmetic for one token takes on the order of a tenth of a millisecond. Yet real systems produce maybe fifty to a hundred tokens per second per request — thousands of times slower than pure arithmetic suggests. The gap is exactly what part five predicted: decode isn't waiting on arithmetic, it's waiting on memory. A seventy-billion-parameter model at sixteen-bit precision is a hundred and forty gigabytes of weights that must move from memory to compute for every token. Divide memory bandwidth by model size and you get the real ceiling on tokens per second — a number you can compute from a spec sheet.

That single calculation explains half the engineering you'll hear about in the coming episodes. Why quantization matters: smaller weights means fewer bytes to move means faster decode, directly proportional. Why batching matters: if you must move all the weights anyway, amortize the trip across fifty concurrent requests. Why speculative decoding exists: use a small model to draft several tokens, and let the big model verify them in one parallel pass — converting serial decode into parallel-ish verification. None of these are tricks anymore; they're the obvious moves once you know where the bottleneck is.

Let's land this.

The full trace, in one breath: text becomes integers through a frozen tokenizer; integers become vectors through a lookup table; vectors flow through a stack of identical blocks where attention mixes context between tokens and feed-forward layers apply stored knowledge; a final projection produces a probability distribution over the vocabulary; a sampler — plain code, not model — picks one token; and the whole thing loops, one full forward pass per generated token, with the KV cache saving you from recomputing the past at the price of gigabytes of memory.

And the three load-bearing takeaways. One: the model is a deterministic function from token sequence to probability distribution; everything that feels like personality or randomness lives in the sampler and the prompt. Two: prefill is parallel and compute-bound, decode is serial and memory-bound — this one asymmetry explains pricing, time to first token, throughput numbers, and most of inference engineering. Three: the KV cache is the hidden protagonist — it's why long context is a memory problem rather than a speed problem, and why prompt caching is one of the few genuine free lunches available to you as an application engineer.

Next episode in this track, we open up attention properly: queries, keys, values, multi-head, causal masking — the actual matrices, small enough numbers that you can hold them in your head. Once you have that, the KV cache episode will feel less like trivia and more like an inevitability.

You now know what happens when you press enter. Most people building on these models don't. That gap is the point of this whole track.
