We are all racing to put AI coding into production, and the narrative that dominates the conversation is simple: write more loops, throw more agents at the problem, configure more linters and magic prompts, and soon your software will build itself without anyone reading a single line.

But something is breaking under this pressure.

Companies that have no business having production outages are having them — not because of infrastructure failure, but because coding agents vibed a little too hard and nobody was reading the code. Pull-request review quality is down. Incidents are up. Bugs per developer are up. The correlation is strong enough that even if it isn't a smoking gun yet, it feels directionally true to anyone who has actually shipped real code with these tools.

The people pushing the "just token harder" narrative are genuinely smart, and they have real respect for the technology. But the most cynical take is that this is yet another excuse to pump more venture capital into what amounts to a slop cannon — a system that produces volume at the expense of quality, with no feedback loop to correct the long-term decay.

## This is not a skill issue

The pushback when you raise these concerns is always the same: it's your fault. If token-maxxing isn't working for you, you're holding it wrong. If the code quality is degrading, you just need better harness engineering. If the codebase is falling apart, you haven't prompted it correctly.

But here is what the author of this piece has learned after going deep on coding agents for a very long time: no amount of harness engineering or loop optimization can solve what is fundamentally a model-training issue.

Models are good at some things — solving one-off problems, vibe-coding a marketing site, even writing brilliant refactors when you tell them exactly what to do. But they are not good at maintaining and improving codebase quality over time. That specific form of maintainability — where changing one part of the system stops breaking three other parts — is something models degrade rather than preserve.

## There are no good benchmarks for codebase quality

The reason we can't prove this with data is that today's coding benchmarks don't measure maintainability at all. They measure whether a model can write a patch that passes a specific test, and nothing more. There is no penalty for bad design. No penalty for eroding codebase quality over time. No penalty for introducing patterns that will make future changes expensive and fragile.

Consider a typical SWE-bench task: the model gets a bug report and a codebase, writes a patch, and then the benchmark slaps a hidden test on top. If the tests pass, we win. The model could have solved it with a single clean line, or it could have wrapped everything in try-catches, hardcoded values, and lazy type casts that undermine the entire benefit of having a type system. Both get the same reward.

## The lights-off factory doesn't work

The lights-off software factory — where humans never read code, never review, never intervene — sounds elegant in theory. You invest all your effort into testing, sandboxes, automated review, monitoring, and rollout, and let the agents cook unattended.

But anyone who has tried this seriously knows how it ends. Sooner or later, you find an issue gnarly enough that no amount of prompting can solve. You have to dig into the codebase you stopped reading three months ago. Your site was down. Your users were pissed. And you spent two weeks in VS Code plumbing out all the patterns by hand because the agent spaghetti was too much to unwind.

The company that ran this experiment and shipped 75 percent of their code with agents? Their pull-request review quality dropped by 25 percent more comments, 22 percent longer comments, and 31 percent of PRs skipping review entirely. Their incidents went up 242 percent per PR and 57 percent overall. Their bugs per developer went up 54 percent.

## What actually works: four phases of planning

If you want to use AI coding agents without setting your codebase on fire, you need to front-load alignment. The four phases that work are product review, system architecture, program design, and vertical slices.

Start with a product review doc — a short, semi-structured document that pins down what you are building and why, ideally with rough HTML mockups instead of paragraphs. Then align on system architecture — how services, endpoints, schemas, and stores talk to each other, using visualizations like sequence diagrams to maximize human-agent communication bandwidth.

Then do program design — and this is the criminally underemphasized one. Before anyone writes implementation, go one level down from architecture into the shape of code: the types, method signatures, program layout, and call stacks. Light visualizations in pseudocode — call-stack trees, file-tree diffs, type signatures — are decisions you would otherwise be making implicitly during the most expensive possible moment: code review.

Finally, build in vertical slices or tracer bullets — working, testable increments that touch the full stack, not horizontal layers where you can't touch the solution until everything is done.

## You probably feel like you have too many pull requests

You don't have too many PRs. You have too many bad PRs.

A great PR is a joy to review. Clean code, clear decisions, nothing to figure out. A PR that needs even 20 percent rework is an intellectual and emotional burden on both the submitter and the reviewer. Most AI oneshot PRs trend closer to 50 percent rework, because the model vibed the architecture without the human steering that would have caught the problems early.

The fix is not to automate review into oblivion. The fix is to keep humans in the loop for the high-leverage phases where an agent misunderstanding your intent is expensive, and let the agents cook only on the slices where you can resteer early and cheaply.

## Four closing pieces of advice

If you want to move 2 to 3 times faster without setting your codebase on fire, start here: learn the constraints well through hands-on work with models. Optimize your systems within the arena of those constraints. Seek leverage where the model's strengths align with your workflow. And read the dang code — when maintainability has no fast oracle for reinforcement learning, the human judgment loop stays irreplaceable.

The hype will tell you the lights-off factory is coming. The discipline says: for now, the judge is you.
