Every rule you ever added to your CLAUDE.md or AGENTS.md felt necessary at the time. The agent did something wrong, you patched the file, and you moved on. Months later, the file is three hundred lines long, your token costs are up, and the agent is somehow worse than before. That is not a failure of intent — it is a failure of maintenance, and Addy Osmani, a longtime advocate for agent configuration practices, has been thinking hard about why it happens and what to do about it.

The core problem, as Osmani frames it, is that agent configuration rots. Models improve, harnesses add capabilities, codebases evolve, and the instructions written for an older system stay behind, quietly occupying context. An Anthropic recommendation that has been circulating is to periodically delete your CLAUDE.md and your skills — perhaps every couple of months — and rebuild only what still earns its place. The fear most people have is that quality will drop. What Osmani found when he actually ran the experiment is that it often does not, and sometimes things get better.

The data backing this intuition is more grounded than most practitioners realize. A June study of one hundred popular repositories found lint-related leakage in 62 percent, context bloat in 42 percent, and skill leakage in 35 percent of files. Anthropic itself removed more than 80 percent of Claude Code's system prompt for its Claude 5 generation models and measured no loss on internal coding evaluations. The lesson is not that instructions are worthless but that their value expires. An instruction that was compensating for a behavior the model has since fixed is now just noise wearing a rule's clothing.

The other finding worth sitting with comes from a recent paper on personalized skills. The intuitive hypothesis is that a skill built from your own interaction history — your preferences for small changes, your testing conventions, your distaste for unrelated refactors — should outperform a generic one. The paper found otherwise. A skill drawn from one developer's history performed roughly as well as a skill borrowed from a stranger. A generic skill derived from many developers performed better overall. Where personalization did help was in specificity around recurring tasks: concrete examples for a specific domain, not prose preferences about formatting.

This reframes what a good configuration file is actually for. The distinction Osmani draws is between things the model cannot easily infer from the code — expensive commands, architectural boundaries, safety rules, unusual project conventions — and generic advice about writing clean code, which the model already knows and will likely ignore anyway. A related study found that prose summaries of a codebase answered four out of 45 behavioral questions about it, while the source code itself answered 27 of 45. The implication is pointed: give the agent real code, not descriptions of real code.

The practical audit process Osmani uses now is straightforward. He runs the Claude Code slash-doctor command every few weeks — a configuration checkup that surfaces unused skills, over-specified files, slow hooks, and accumulated cruft. He reviews memory separately with slash-memory, because auto-memory accumulates stale preferences even when the project files look clean. When he ran doctor on his own setup, he found skills he had completely forgotten installing, including a collection of anti-slop writing experiments from months earlier that were presumably firing, conflicting, or doing nothing at all.

One useful thought experiment he recommends is to instruct the agent to complete a real task using only the raw model and harness, with local skills disabled. If the output is acceptable, you have learned something about which skills are doing real work and which ones you installed as insurance against a problem that no longer exists. The instinct to keep configuration files as a permanent crutch — the fear that removing anything will cause a regression — is worth examining, because it is often not the file protecting you but your reluctance to trust that the underlying model has improved.

The team and organizational framing is also worth noting. Individual engineer skills may produce inconsistent value, but skills that capture a team's engineering culture, compliance rules, internal tooling quirks, and review conventions can compound across people. The abstraction that works best at small scale is different from the one that works at org scale. At the personal level, the right target is lean and specific — short decision guides, not knowledge bases. At team scale, shared conventions and concrete examples start to justify the overhead.

The summary is simple enough to carry into practice: install a skill when it solves a specific problem, archive it when that problem goes away, run doctor on a cadence, and ask every remaining instruction to justify its cost before it earns another month in your context window.
