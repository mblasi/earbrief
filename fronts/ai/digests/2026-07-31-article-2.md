Itamar Friedman just published a sharp intervention in the debate over whether engineers should read the code their agents generate, and his answer is both sides are right, because they're answering different questions.

The piece dropped on X two days ago, and it matters because Friedman runs Qodo, formerly CodiumAI, one of the companies building the review agents everyone is betting on to make dark factories safe. When the founder of a code review tool says human review still has to stay in the loop, that's not a business pitch. That's field data.

Here's the setup. Uncle Bob Martin says he doesn't read the code his agents write anymore. He surrounds them with a gauntlet: unit tests, acceptance tests, QA procedures, coverage metrics, mutation testing, and periodic manual validation. He reviews the specifications and the evidence, not every generated line. Dex Horthy, on the other hand, published two articles arguing the opposite: turn the lights back on. Read the dang code. His team tried the lights-off factory, and after a few months they were spending weeks reverse-engineering a codebase they'd stopped reading, until rewriting large parts of it was easier than continuing to repair it.

Friedman says the tension comes from treating code and the codebase as the same thing. They're not. An individual piece of generated code is increasingly disposable. You can regenerate it, replace it, or rewrite it cheaply. The codebase is not disposable. The codebase is the accumulated architecture of the company, the tribal knowledge, the pull request history. It holds years of decisions, constraints, exceptions, interfaces, conventions, and hard-earned lessons. A locally disposable implementation can still make a globally permanent mess.

Uncle Bob is optimizing for confidence in an output. Dex is optimizing for the continued integrity of a system. Both matter, and the mistake is picking one side.

The deeper problem Friedman identifies is that tests are great at telling you whether an endpoint returns the expected response, whether an existing behavior broke, whether latency and security still hold. They're much weaker at answering questions like: does this responsibility belong in this layer? Will this abstraction survive the next five requirements? Did the change quietly increase coupling? Will the next engineer understand why this design exists? Are we preserving the architecture, or just preserving today's behavior?

A locally correct change can still make the whole system worse. Tests give feedback in seconds. Bad architecture sends the bill in months or years. That's not a small gap in the harness. It's a fundamental difficulty: building a fast, reliable oracle for maintainability. More review agents can raise the floor, catching bugs, security issues, style violations, duplicated code, known anti-patterns, and deviations from documented standards. But they don't necessarily raise or eliminate the ceiling. As Friedman puts it, if the model could always recognize the most maintainable implementation, it probably would have generated that implementation in the first place. We don't yet have a fast, reliable verifier for the evolving quality of a complex codebase.

Friedman's resolution is what he calls progressive autonomy. For familiar, low-risk changes with clear requirements, strong tests, documented standards, a limited blast radius, and good observability, agents should move almost autonomously. For novel, architectural, security-sensitive, or high-impact changes, humans should stay deeply involved. But their attention should move toward the decisions where judgment has the highest leverage: intent, architecture, program design, evidence, and exceptions. Review shouldn't be applied uniformly to every generated line. It should be proportional to the uncertainty and the strength of the evidence. Weak evidence should demand deeper inspection. Strong evidence should earn more autonomy.

The mechanism for making that work is moving human judgment earlier, before thousands of generated lines make changing direction expensive. Friedman breaks serious work into four stages: product requirements, system architecture, program design, and vertical slices. First, align on the problem and the outcome you want for the user. Then align on how services, endpoints, schemas, queues, and data stores interact. Then go one level deeper into the shape of the program itself: the types, method signatures, call stacks, file layout, and who owns which responsibility. Only then does implementation begin.

And even implementation shouldn't land as one enormous generated change. Vertical slices matter because agents tend to produce horizontal plans. They build the whole database layer, then the service layer, then the API, then the frontend. You end up with thousands of lines before anyone can interact with the feature as a complete system. A vertical slice gives you evidence sooner. It lets you re-steer the agent after one hundred or two hundred lines instead of discovering the wrong design after two thousand.

Friedman frames this with a thought experiment. The old interview question gives us two disks: one with the code, one with the tests. The classic answer was always the tests, because code is just one implementation of the system, but the tests preserve what the system is supposed to do. The AI era reveals a third disk. This one holds the intent of the system: its architecture, its standards, its constraints, its important design decisions, and the accumulated judgment of the engineers who built it. Tests tell us what the system does. The third disk tells us what kind of system we're trying to preserve. It includes product requirements, service boundaries, API contracts, architectural principles, security policies, ownership, coding standards, known exceptions, and the reasoning behind the decisions that mattered. Without this context, agents can keep producing code that works while slowly destroying the properties that made the system maintainable.

The durable asset isn't just the tests. It's the full system of intent, constraints, context, evidence, and governance around the code. And over time, every repeated human judgment should become a candidate for codification. When reviewers keep rejecting the same pattern, that pattern should become a rule. When a failure reaches production, the lesson should become a test, a policy, or a new verification workflow. When an architectural decision gets made, agents should receive it as persistent context instead of rediscovering it on every task. Human review isn't the opposite of automation. It's how we teach the governance system what still needs to be automated.

The goal Friedman is reaching for is nonlinear human leverage, borrowing Gergely Orosz's framing from a conversation with Dex. The objective isn't maximum autonomy. It's systems where reliability, deployment volume, and operational scale can grow much faster than the number of people needed to manage them. As agents generate more code, human attention shouldn't scale linearly with every line produced. But human judgment shouldn't disappear either. It should concentrate where it has the highest leverage. The success metric isn't how many pull requests got generated without a human. It's how much reliable engineering output the organization can produce for every unit of scarce human judgment.

The piece closes with the synthesis: Uncle Bob is describing the destination, where code becomes disposable and constraints and verification become the source of trust. Dex is describing the bridge we still have to cross, where today's models and harnesses can't reliably protect long-term maintainability without human judgment. The mistake is picking one side. We should build toward Uncle Bob's world while operating with Dex's honesty about where we actually are today. The code may be disposable. The health of the codebase is not.
