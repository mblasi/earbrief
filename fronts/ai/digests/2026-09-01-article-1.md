Most people use AI at five to ten percent of what it can actually do. Anatoli Kopadze, a software engineer whose study of process optimization at one of Denmark's best universities pays off in a radically different way, has been watching the field pivot from talking about loops — single agents improving one thing on repeat — to talking about graphs of loops. The shift happened quietly, and it marks the moment when workflow design became the skill that separates doing one job from designing how a hundred of them get done.

Kopadze's article on Graph Engineering is stripped of jargon while being precise about structure, because the vocabulary is mercifully small. A graph is a plan for your AI work drawn out so you can see the dependencies. It answers two questions: which jobs need to happen, and which job has to wait for which. There are only two building blocks, and getting them straight fixes most of the confusion that newcomers create.

A node is a box — one job, one agent doing one task, with one thing going in and one thing coming out. Researching a competitor, writing a draft, checking a claim. An edge is an arrow, and it means one job needs what another job produced, so it has to wait for it. Nodes do the thinking. Edges carry the results. Kopadze points out that the arrow only counts when something real actually passes along it, which immediately surfaces a problem most people build into their workflows without seeing it: fake edges. A linear workflow where you write an agent as do A, then B, then C, then D is technically a graph already, just the most redundant one possible — a single chain where every node has one arrow in and one arrow out.

The test to find fake edges is deceptively simple. Walk through your workflow step by step, and at each step ask one thing: does this step actually need the result of the one before it? If yes, the edge is real. Keep the order. If no, the edge is fake, and the wait is time you are throwing away for free. The example Kopadze gives is review file A for bugs, then review file B for bugs. It reads like a sequence, but the check on file B never looks at what file A returned. Running them side by side, the whole thing finishes in the time of the slower single file, not the two added together. You will find two or three of these fake edges in almost any workflow you draw, and every one of them is latency you can delete for free by drawing the dependency correctly.

The shape that pays is called the diamond, and it is the pattern that appears again and again in serious agent systems. The work splits, several workers dig side by side, something checks what they found, and everything merges back into one answer. In graph terms that is called fan out, reduce, synthesize, and it is close to the only pattern you need this year. A linear workflow with forty steps has forty points of sequential failure and the latency of all forty added together. The same forty jobs drawn as a graph have only as many real dependencies as actually exist, usually three to five, and finish at the speed of your slowest layer, not the sum of everything. That is the difference between a job that takes five minutes and one that takes fifteen seconds running the exact same work.

The reason this is not cosmetic, Kopadze argues, is that the model was never the bottleneck. The line you drew was. The skill of graph engineering is redrawing that line. Take your linear workflow, and for each arrow ask the fake-edge question. Cut the arrows that carry no data, and the line collapses into something wider. A few independent jobs that can all run at once, feeding one job that needs them all. The pattern has run critical systems for thirty years, which is exactly why you want to trust it with your work.
