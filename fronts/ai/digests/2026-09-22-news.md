Today OpenAI shipped two major milestones that signal a fundamental shift in how we think about model outputs and decision-making at scale. GPT-6 Sol and Luna represent the next phase of reasoning models, built on better scaling laws for test-time compute. And in parallel, TypeSafe AI's Jev continues proving that a new category of model—decision models—is emerging as the practical alternative to general text-generation models.

The pattern is clear now: frontier labs have stopped optimizing for text output. They're optimizing for correct decisions. GPT-6 Sol emphasizes reasoning trajectories, letting you see the model's step-by-step work on complex problems. Luna is the production variant, optimized for latency and cost while maintaining reasoning quality. Both use inference-time scaling: more compute at test time means better answers without retraining.

Simon Willison covered Jev's plugin ecosystem this week. The llm-typesafe plugin lets you use Jev for yes-no questions, choice routing, and scoring tasks without any LLM hallucination risk. You send a question, you get back a floating-point score or a category, with confidence. This isn't new conceptually—decision models exist in recommendation systems—but productizing them as a first-class LLM variant is novel.

The implications are massive. A typical agent spends sixty percent of its inference budget on routing decisions. Routing which tool to call, which service owns a request, which branch of logic to take. Text-generation models are overkill for this. Jev is five times cheaper and a hundred times faster. Teams are already cloning it: six variants shipped in two days after Jev's announcement.

OpenAI's mathematics advisory group announcement from last week is now in motion. They're specifically recruiting mathematicians to work on how AI can contribute to formal proof verification and mathematical discovery. This is post-training for mathematical reasoning, not general chat. It signals that the next frontier is reasoning depth, not breadth.

Gemini 3.8 Flash and 3.8 Flash Cyber keep iterating on the Gemini stack. Flash is the general-purpose fast variant. Cyber is specialized for adversarial inputs and red-teaming scenarios. This mirrors TypeSafe's approach: build specialized models for specific decision problems rather than one model for everything.

The GPU infrastructure conversation shifted this week too. Training a seventy billion parameter model requires predictable, sustained GPU utilization over seventy-two hours. That constraint shapes how labs choose between pre-training, fine-tuning, and inference-time scaling. The math is shifting: inference-time test scaling is becoming cheaper than fine-tuning for downstream tasks.

The emerging pattern: models are no longer optimized for "understanding." They're optimized for the specific downstream task—routing, scoring, reasoning, generation. Each gets its own model category, its own scaling laws, its own cost structure.
