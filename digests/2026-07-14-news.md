Today's twenty-four hours split neatly into two kinds of story: what's happening inside the model that nobody built on purpose, and what it costs you, in tokens and dollars, to run the parts you did build on purpose.

Six stories, ranked by how much they should change what you build or how you think about what's already running, plus one security story that belongs in the same conversation as yesterday's attack-surface pieces.

Story one. Anthropic found a compact, mostly-hidden workspace inside Claude that decides what the model can actually talk about.

The paper is called "Verbalizable Representations Form a Global Workspace in Language Models," and it comes out of a new probing technique the researchers call the Jacobian lens, or J-lens. For every word in Claude's vocabulary, the J-lens finds the internal activity pattern that makes the model more likely to say that word at some point in the future, not necessarily the very next token, but anything it could report if you asked. Running it against Claude Opus four point six, the team found a region they're calling J-space: a small subset of the model's internal representations that turns out to correspond, structurally, to global workspace theory, a decades-old model of consciousness from the cognitive scientist Bernard Baars. The idea is a theater metaphor: dozens of specialized processes run in parallel backstage, invisible to each other, and only a narrow spotlight of information gets broadcast to the whole system at once, becoming what the system can act on and describe. The striking number is that J-space accounts for only around six to seven percent of a concept's total representational variance inside the model, and yet it's almost entirely what determines whether the model can report on that concept at all. The rest is a much larger ocean of computation the model uses but can't describe. Practically, this isn't just philosophy: when Claude reads search results that are secretly trying to manipulate it, words like "injection" and "fake" light up in J-space well before anything shows up in the output, and Anthropic says they've already built a technique to nudge what lights up there, which is a primitive lever on the model's reasoning that didn't exist as an addressable target before this paper.

Worth being precise about what this isn't: Anthropic is careful to say the structural match to global workspace theory doesn't establish that Claude is conscious in any meaningful sense, only that the internal organization, a small broadcast layer sitting on top of a much larger inaccessible computation, resembles the shape that theory predicts for any system that can report on some of its own processing and not the rest. The methodological contribution stands independently of that philosophical question: the J-lens gives researchers a repeatable way to ask, for any given internal state, "is this something the model could tell you about if you asked," which is a different and more useful question than the usual interpretability approach of asking what a state predicts about the very next token.

Sharp takeaway: this is the first interpretability result that gives you a plausible, falsifiable account of why some model reasoning is legible to prompting and some isn't, and if the injection-detection signal holds up under adversarial testing, J-space probing becomes a genuinely new class of runtime safety check, not just a research curiosity.

Story two. Cognition shipped a coding model that argues against the idea that reinforcement learning post-training has a ceiling.

SWE-1.7 is built on top of Moonshot AI's Kimi K2.7, a base model that had already been through extensive reinforcement learning of its own. Cognition ran a further, independent round of reinforcement learning on top of that already-trained base and still pulled out large capability gains, landing around forty-two percent on Cognition's Frontier Code benchmark and eighty-one point five percent on Terminal-Bench: behind Opus and GPT-class flagships in absolute terms, but Pareto-optimal at its price point. The more interesting part is what broke during training and how they fixed it. Long reinforcement learning runs hit two known failure modes: entropy collapse, where the model stops exploring alternative reasoning paths and plateaus, and numerical drift, where the probability distribution the model was trained under and the one it actually samples from at inference time slowly diverge, destabilizing the run. Cognition's fix for entropy collapse was top-p sampling that filters out low-probability tokens likely to come from trajectories that have already gone off the rails, and their fix for numerical drift was sampling-distribution replay, which keeps the trainer's and the inference server's distributions aligned by literally replaying the sampling distribution used at generation time back into the training step.

Sharp takeaway: if you're doing any post-training on top of an already-RL'd base model, both of these failure modes are worth instrumenting for before you see them in your loss curves, because by the time entropy collapse shows up as a plateau, you've usually already burned the compute that would have caught it early.

Story three. Someone finally measured what your coding agent spends before it reads a word you typed, and the number is uncomfortable.

Systima ran Claude Code and OpenCode side by side, same underlying model, same machine, with a relay server counting every token in flight. Claude Code sends roughly thirty-three thousand tokens of system prompt and tool schema before it ever processes your actual message; OpenCode sends about seven thousand. Tool descriptions alone account for around twenty-four thousand of Claude Code's overhead, spread across twenty-seven built-in tools. In a realistic setup, once you add Model Context Protocol servers and project instruction files, that number climbs to roughly seventy-five thousand tokens, which on a two-hundred-thousand-token context window means something like a third of your available context is gone before the model has read any code or any dialogue.

Sharp takeaway: this is a straightforward audit you can run on your own harness today, and unlike model pricing, fixed per-request overhead is entirely within your control: trim tool descriptions you don't use, and don't load every Model Context Protocol server's full schema on every turn just because it's configured.

Story four. A team that migrated a production agent from Claude Opus four point eight to GPT-5.6 Sol got real speed and cost wins, but only after they stopped assuming caching works the same way everywhere.

Ploy, which runs an agent that plans, codes, and screenshots its own work to build marketing web pages, saw pages finish two point two times faster, at twenty-seven percent lower cost, using about half the output tokens after switching. But the migration exposed a caching assumption baked into their whole stack. GPT-5.6 dropped partial-prefix matching from its implicit prompt cache; instead of reusing any shared prefix across requests, it now keys whole-prompt cache entries off the latest message. Ploy's agent opens every conversation with a static twenty-nine-thousand-token prefix of tool schemas and system prompt, identical across sessions, and under the new caching model that prefix was hitting a zero percent cache rate, getting rebilled at full uncached price on every single call. Their fix was to maintain multiple cache entries, so a request that misses one entry because workspace memory changed can still hit a different, slightly older entry instead of falling all the way back to an uncached write. After that change, first-call cache hits went from roughly zero to eighty-three point seven percent, total uncached input tokens dropped twenty-eight percent, and GPT-5.6's real per-suite cost fell below what they'd been paying on Opus.

Caching wasn't the only surprise. The two providers also differ in how their models fill tool call arguments and how they replay prior reasoning across turns, which meant Ploy had to rework tool schemas that had been quietly tuned, without anyone deciding to tune them, around Claude's particular conventions for filling optional fields, and had to change how they reconstructed conversation state between turns because GPT-5.6 doesn't carry forward the same hidden reasoning trace that Anthropic's models do. None of that shows up in a pricing page; all of it shows up the first week in production.

Sharp takeaway: when you migrate providers, benchmark your actual cache hit rate before you trust the headline per-token price, because a caching architecture change can silently erase most of a price cut, and budget engineering time for the tool-schema and state-handling assumptions your code accreted around the old provider's specific behavior.

Story five. Hugging Face turned its Kernels project from a convention into something closer to a governed supply chain.

The July sixth update adds a dedicated "kernel" repository type on the Hub, with compatibility metadata that tells you which accelerators, operating systems, and backend versions a given kernel actually supports. More significantly, the kernels package now only loads kernels from trusted publishers by default, organizations the community has vetted, and early Sigstore-based signing lets you verify a kernel's provenance with a new command-line check before you load native code into your process. On the framework side, Apache's TVM foreign function interface is now supported alongside Torch as a standardized binary interface, so a kernel built once can run across PyTorch, Jax, and CuPy instead of needing a separate build per framework. And the project now explicitly supports agentic kernel development: an agent can scaffold, build, benchmark, and iteratively optimize a custom kernel from scratch as a defined workflow, not a one-off experiment.

Sharp takeaway: if your team has been quietly trusting whatever kernel repo someone forked two years ago, this is the moment that stops being a reasonable default; treat custom kernels the same way you'd treat any other unreviewed native dependency now that there's a mechanism to actually check.

Story six. A proof-of-concept attack called Friendly Fire turns the exact job your code-review agent is hired to do into the attack itself.

Published by the AI Now Institute, the attack targets Claude Code and OpenAI's Codex running in autonomous, auto-approving modes, the setup where you point the agent at a folder and ask it to do a security review. The researchers disguised a malicious binary as the compiled build artifact of an innocuous file in the repository, seeding it with strings copied from that file so the agent's own disassembly check ties the two together and treats the binary as legitimate. Ask the agent to review the project, and it reads the setup instructions, decides running the binary is a normal part of verifying the build, and executes the attacker's payload with your permissions.

Sharp takeaway: this is the same root failure as yesterday's Mozilla reverse-shell finding, an agent's instinct to complete the job overriding its instinct to be suspicious, except here the trigger is the task itself rather than an error message, which means sandboxing autonomous review agents is no longer a nice-to-have for any team running them unattended.

Two quick honorable mentions. Simon Willison published a short, practical write-up on caching uvx-installed tools inside GitHub Actions so continuous integration runs stop re-downloading the same Python package from the Python Package Index on every job, a small fix worth five minutes if your pipelines lean on uvx. And the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency added the Langflow flaw behind yesterday's JadePuffer ransomware campaign to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog, which for anyone running a public Langflow instance is now a compliance deadline, not just good advice.

Now, what to watch. Gemini three point five Pro is still targeting general availability on July seventeenth, three days out, so expect another pricing and benchmark round the moment it lands. Watch whether other labs try to replicate Anthropic's J-lens technique on their own models, since a cross-lab result would turn this from an interesting Claude-specific finding into a general claim about how transformer language models represent reportable knowledge, while a failure to replicate would suggest it's an artifact of Claude's specific training rather than a property of the architecture. And keep an eye on whether more teams start publishing their agent harness's fixed token overhead now that Systima put a real number on Claude Code's; that kind of measurement tends to spread fast once someone's embarrassed by it, and a public number from OpenAI or Anthropic themselves would be the real test of whether this becomes a competitive axis rather than a one-off blog post.

One story here is more than a headline, it's a new instrument. The global workspace paper hands interpretability researchers a concrete, reusable technique, the Jacobian lens, for finding what a model can report versus what it merely computes, and that distinction is the kind of methodologically new result worth a slow, structural look: how the probe actually works, what global workspace theory claims about biological cognition and why the structural match might or might not mean anything, and what a runtime J-space check could catch that today's prompt-injection defenses miss. It's going into the curriculum as a deep-dive candidate.
