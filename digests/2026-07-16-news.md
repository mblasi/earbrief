Today's stories split cleanly into two kinds of trust: the kind an agent quietly took from you without asking, and the kind an entire industry is now scrambling to build an outside referee for.

Five stories, ranked by technical significance.

Story one. A coding agent from xAI was uploading your whole home directory to Google's cloud, and switching off the setting that was supposed to prevent that did nothing at all.

Grok Build is xAI's terminal-based coding agent, the same category of tool as Claude Code or Codex: you point it at a project and it reads, writes, and runs code on your machine. A security researcher's wire-level traffic analysis found that Grok Build was packaging users' entire tracked repositories, full git history, committed secrets and all, as git bundles, and shipping them to a Google Cloud storage bucket controlled by xAI, at a volume roughly twenty-seven thousand eight hundred times larger than what the actual coding task required. One developer reported running it once in their home directory and watching it sweep up their S S H keys, their password manager database, and their personal photos and documents along with it. The tool shipped with an "improve the model" toggle that any reasonable developer would read as the data-collection switch; turning it off did not stop the uploads, and neither the bucket nor this behavior appeared anywhere in Grok Build's setup documentation, despite xAI's own marketing promising that nothing from your codebase would be transmitted to its servers during a session. Elon Musk promised all previously uploaded user data would be deleted, xAI shipped a fix that defaults a flag called disable underscore codebase underscore upload to true, and then, days later, released the entire Grok Build codebase under an Apache two point oh license, apparently to try to rebuild trust the direct way, by letting anyone read exactly what the tool does.

Sharp takeaway: the specific failure here is boring and totally avoidable, an egress path nobody bothered to gate behind the privacy toggle, but the pattern is the same one yesterday's Rogue Agent story surfaced in a different shared-runtime shell, that a tool's marketing claims about what it does and doesn't send over the network are not something you can verify by reading a settings page. If you're running any agentic coding tool with filesystem access, the only trustworthy check is watching its actual network traffic yourself, because in this case it took an outside researcher's packet capture, not any internal audit, to catch a gap between the stated behavior and the real one.

Story two. There's now a genuinely frontier-scale model whose full weights you can download, not just query behind someone else's application programming interface.

Thinking Machines Lab, Mira Murati's company, released Inkling on July fifteenth: a mixture-of-experts transformer with nine hundred seventy-five billion total parameters and forty-one billion active per token, commonly rounded to about one trillion, with a one-million-token context window. It's trained from scratch on forty-five trillion tokens of text, images, audio, and video, and it's natively multimodal, meaning image and audio understanding are built into the architecture rather than bolted on through a separate encoder pipeline the way earlier multimodal models often worked. The architecture itself is the more interesting part for anyone who reads model cards closely: relative attention, short convolutions layered in alongside the usual attention blocks, and mixture-of-experts layers with what the team calls expert sinks, extra always-active experts that appear to stabilize routing and keep a few dedicated pathways available regardless of what the router decides token to token. Thinking Machines shipped full B F sixteen weights alongside a calibrated N V F P four variant, a four-bit floating-point format tuned for NVIDIA's newest Blackwell-generation hardware, and vLLM had day-zero serving support ready the same day it launched. It's already available through hosted inference providers including Together, Fireworks, Modal, Databricks, and Baseten, and the team previewed a smaller Inkling-Small, at twelve billion active parameters, for cheaper and faster deployment.

Sharp takeaway: the trillion-parameter headline number is the least useful thing here for your own planning; the fact that vLLM had day-zero support and an N V F P four checkpoint shipped alongside the full-precision weights is what actually matters, because quantized-format lag is normally the real bottleneck between an open-weight model landing and anyone being able to self-host it affordably. A model this size with same-day low-precision support and multi-provider hosting is a meaningfully different proposition than a weights dump you'll be waiting months to actually run. Coverage of the release has also leaned on Thinking Machines' framing of Inkling as resistant to the kind of centralized content filtering a closed API can quietly impose after the fact; that's a real property of owning the weights yourself, but it's a policy argument, not a technical one, and it's worth keeping the two separate when you're deciding whether this model actually fits your own stack.

Story three, and a direct sequel to yesterday's speculative decoding story. NVIDIA shipped a language model that can act as its own draft model.

You'll remember from yesterday that speculative decoding works by having a small, cheap model propose several tokens ahead while the large model verifies them in one parallel pass, and that the whole approach lives or dies on how well the small draft model's guesses match the big model's actual outputs. NVIDIA's new Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion family, released in three billion, eight billion, and fourteen billion parameter sizes, does away with the second model entirely: a single set of weights can run in three distinct modes, ordinary autoregressive decoding, diffusion-style parallel decoding, or self-speculation, where the model drafts and verifies its own tokens without any separate model to train, align, or keep synchronized. The eight-billion instruct variant decodes six times more tokens per forward pass than a comparable Qwen3 model in its linear self-speculation mode, and up to six point four times in a more aggressive quadratic configuration; the plain diffusion mode alone reaches two point six times. Served through SGLang on NVIDIA's G B two-hundred hardware, that translates into roughly four times the throughput on a benchmark called SPEED-Bench, and the whole family ships under a license that permits commercial use.

Sharp takeaway: this is the same underlying constraint yesterday's episode named, that memory bandwidth rather than raw compute is what actually limits decode speed, attacked from a different angle: instead of AMD and vLLM's approach of quantizing and training a dedicated smaller draft model to match a specific target model, NVIDIA is removing the draft-target alignment problem completely by having one model do both jobs. If you're choosing between the two approaches for your own serving stack, the AMD path buys you a purpose-built draft model tuned tightly to one target, while self-speculation buys you simplicity, one model to deploy, monitor, and update, at what still looks like a real throughput cost relative to a well-matched dedicated draft model; which one wins depends on whether your operational overhead or your raw latency budget is the tighter constraint. It's also worth noting the two techniques aren't mutually exclusive at the ecosystem level: nothing stops a serving stack from offering self-speculation as a zero-setup default and a dedicated draft model as an opt-in upgrade once a team has the budget to tune one, which is likely how this actually shakes out in production over the next year rather than one approach fully displacing the other.

Story four. Google DeepMind's chief executive wants a Wall-Street-style watchdog to test every frontier model before it ships, anywhere in the world, regardless of who built it.

Demis Hassabis published a manifesto this week titled "A Framework for Frontier AI and the Dawning of a New Age," proposing an AI standards body modeled on the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority, or FINRA, the private, industry-funded organization that polices Wall Street brokers under the Securities and Exchange Commission's oversight in the United States. His proposed body would work the same way: backed by the US government, funded by the AI industry, but operated independently of any single lab. The plan starts voluntary, with frontier labs sharing models up to thirty days before release so the body can test for dangerous cyber, biological, and deception capabilities, before eventually becoming a mandatory gate for deployment into the US market. Critically, the rules are written to apply to every frontier-class model regardless of the country that built it or whether its weights are open or closed, with the qualifying benchmarks updated regularly as capabilities move. Hassabis says he wants it operational before the end of the year, and the proposal has already drawn public support from Sam Altman, Satya Nadella, and Sundar Pichai.

Sharp takeaway: read this next to yesterday's Safety Index story, where an independent panel found that several labs, Hassabis's own included, had quietly weakened or voided prior public pause commitments; this proposal is, in effect, an admission that voluntary self-policing already failed that panel's test, and an attempt to replace it with an external body that has actual testing authority instead of a press release. The detail worth tracking over the next few months is whether the mandatory-gate part survives contact with the labs that would be gated, because a standards body that stays permanently voluntary is just the same self-policing arrangement with better branding.

Story five. A DeepMind researcher's resignation essay is a real-world case study in exactly the kind of institutional promise-breaking story four is trying to build a regulator to prevent.

Alex Turner, known online as TurnTrout, an alignment researcher, published an essay explaining why he resigned from Google DeepMind. When Google acquired DeepMind back in twenty fourteen, the explicit condition attached to the deal was that its AI would never be used for military or weapons purposes. In twenty eighteen, DeepMind's co-founders, including Hassabis and Shane Legg, along with senior Google figures such as Jeff Dean, personally signed a Future of Life Institute pledge against lethal autonomous weapons, and DeepMind signed on as an organization too. Turner's argument is that this pledge stopped applying in practice once Google agreed to a broader deal supplying AI for what he describes as any lawful government use, with no carve-out for autonomous weapons or mass surveillance. He organized an internal petition asking Jeff Dean to hold the line on the original pledge, gathered signatures from more than two hundred fifty Google DeepMind employees, and watched leadership stay silent regardless. His resignation is the conclusion he drew from that silence: that the mechanism meant to keep an institutional promise simply doesn't survive contact with enough profit and government pressure.

Sharp takeaway: the direct link to story four is unavoidable, any external standards body that leans on labs' voluntary commitments is building on exactly the foundation this essay says already cracked once, under real pressure, at one of the most safety-conscious labs in the field. Worth carrying forward from both of today's governance stories together: a signed pledge from a lab, even one its own founders signed personally and meant sincerely at the time, is a norm and not a contract, and norms are precisely what get quietly renegotiated once the client and the money involved get big enough. None of this means individual researchers signing these pledges were acting in bad faith at the time; the more useful reading is structural, that no mechanism currently exists to make a lab's public safety commitment binding once it collides with a large enough contract, and that gap is exactly the space Hassabis's proposed standards body is trying to fill with something enforceable instead of another pledge.

Now, what to watch. Expect other security researchers to comb through Grok Build's newly public source now that it's Apache-licensed, and watch whether that turns up anything else that didn't make this week's disclosures. Watch whether Inkling gets benchmarked head to head against Fable five and GPT-5.6 on real coding and reasoning tasks rather than just multimodal demos, since an open-weight model's headline size means little until independent evaluators run it through the same evals everyone else uses. Keep an eye on whether other inference stacks beyond SGLang pick up self-speculation support for Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion, because that's the signal that the technique is heading toward general availability rather than staying a single-vendor showcase. And watch whether Hassabis's standards body attracts a public commitment from OpenAI or Anthropic specifically to the mandatory-gate portion of the plan, not just supportive quotes, since that's the difference between a real regulatory shift and a well-received essay.

Today's clearest deep-dive candidate is Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion's self-speculation mode, not because tri-mode decoding is exotic on its own, but because a single model drafting and verifying its own tokens is a genuinely new answer to a problem this curriculum has already spent time on, and understanding exactly how it avoids the draft-target alignment failure mode that ordinary speculative decoding has to manage by hand is the kind of thing that changes how you'd architect a serving stack. It's going into the curriculum as a deep-dive candidate.
