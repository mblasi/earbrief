Last time in this track we traced a request end to end and I made you a promise. I said that inside every transformer block there are exactly two things happening: attention, where tokens talk to each other, and a feed-forward network, where each token thinks in private. And I said attention was where context happens, but I waved my hands at the mechanism and told you we'd come back. This is us coming back. By the end of this episode you'll be able to compute an attention step with numbers small enough to hold in your head, you'll know exactly why long context is quadratic before we even get to the memory problem, and you'll understand what grouped-query attention is actually buying when you read it on a model card.

Let's start with the problem attention exists to solve, because the mechanism only makes sense once you feel the need for it.

Recall from last time: after the embedding lookup, every token is a vector, a point in a high-dimensional space, and that starting point encodes the token's context-free dictionary meaning. The word "bank" starts at the same place whether the sentence is about rivers or money. The whole job of the layers is to move each token's vector to where it belongs given the words around it. But here's the catch that makes this hard. The feed-forward network processes each token completely independently. It never looks left or right. So if the layers are going to use context, there has to be one specific place where a token is allowed to reach out to the other tokens and pull in what it needs. That place, the only place in the entire architecture where information moves between positions, is attention. Everything else in the transformer treats your tokens as a bag of independent vectors. Attention is the sole channel through which they become a sequence.

So what does "reaching out and pulling in information" actually mean, mechanically?

Here's the metaphor that maps cleanly onto the math, and then we'll do the math. Think of a library, or honestly, think of a search. Every token gets to do three things. It gets to ask a question — that's its query. It gets to advertise what it's about — that's its key. And it gets to offer up some content if selected — that's its value. Query, key, value. The word "bank," sitting there ambiguous, emits a query that effectively says "I'm a noun that needs a topic — is anyone here about geography or about finance?" Meanwhile the word "river," a few positions back, is advertising a key that says "I'm about water and landscape." When bank's query lines up well with river's key, bank pulls in river's value, and the value is the actual information that shifts bank toward the riverbank region of the space. That's one attention step. Every token does this simultaneously, each one asking its own question and getting its own custom blend of everyone else's values.

Now, where do the query, the key, and the value come from? This is the part people skip, and it's the crux. They are all three derived from the same input vector, by multiplying it through three separate learned weight matrices. Call them W-query, W-key, and W-value. Each token's vector goes in; out come three new vectors, its query, its key, and its value. These three matrices are the actual parameters — they're what the model learned during training. The model was never told what a query should look like. It discovered, over trillions of tokens, that projecting the input one way to make a query and another way to make a key, such that relevant tokens produce queries and keys that point in similar directions, is a good way to reduce prediction error. The library metaphor is something we impose afterward to make sense of it. The model just found three matrices that work.

One subtlety here trips up almost everyone, so let's kill it now: why are the key and the value separate? Why not just use one vector both to be matched against and to be handed over? Because advertising and delivering are different jobs. The key is how a token markets itself for retrieval — the dimensions that make it findable. The value is what it actually contributes once found. A token might advertise "I'm about finance" with its key, in whatever compact form makes matching efficient, but the value it hands over could be a much richer bundle of information that the receiving token folds into itself. Splitting the two lets the model optimize findability and contribution independently, and — this matters in a second — it's the key and the value, not the query, that get cached during generation, because those are what past tokens offer to future ones. The query is a throwaway; you compute it, use it once, discard it.

Let's put numbers on it, because I promised you numbers.

Imagine a toy model where each token vector has just four dimensions, and we have a three-word sequence: "the," "cat," "sat." So we have three input vectors, each with four numbers. We multiply each one by W-query to get three query vectors, by W-key to get three key vectors, by W-value to get three value vectors. To keep it holdable, say the query and key vectors also have four dimensions. Now the core operation. For the word "sat" to figure out what to attend to, we take its query vector and we compute its dot product with every key vector in the sequence — the key from "the," the key from "cat," and its own key. A dot product is just multiply-the-matching-numbers-and-add-them-up; it's a single number that measures how aligned two vectors are. So "sat" gets three numbers, one score per token, measuring how much its question matches each token's advertisement. High score, strong match. That's it. That's the heart of attention: a query dotted against every key, producing one relevance score per position.

Make it painfully concrete for a moment. Suppose "sat's" query, dotted against the three keys, comes out to two point one for "cat," zero point four for "the," and one point six for its own key. Three plain numbers. "Cat" scored highest, which is what you'd hope — the thing that sat is the cat. Nothing mysterious is happening; it is literally multiply-and-add producing a bigger number when two vectors point the same way. The entire intelligence of the operation lives in the weight matrices that produced those queries and keys in the first place. The dot product itself is grade-school arithmetic. Hold onto that, because it demystifies the whole thing: attention is a learned similarity search implemented with the cheapest operation in the building.

Now three small but load-bearing things happen to those scores before they're used.

First, scaling. Those raw dot-product scores have a statistical problem. The more dimensions your query and key vectors have, the larger the dot products tend to get, purely as a matter of arithmetic — you're summing more terms, so the total spreads out further from zero. Concretely, if the numbers going in have a variance of one, the dot product of two vectors with, say, one hundred and twenty-eight dimensions comes out with a variance of one hundred and twenty-eight. Why does that matter? Because the next step is a softmax, and softmax is exquisitely sensitive to the scale of its inputs. Feed it numbers that are spread far apart and it saturates: it hands essentially all the weight to the single largest score and crushes everything else to near zero. A saturated softmax has almost no gradient, so the model can't learn. The fix is embarrassingly clean: divide every score by the square root of the key dimension. If the variance was one hundred and twenty-eight, dividing by the square root of one hundred and twenty-eight brings the variance back to one, right where softmax behaves. This is why it's called scaled dot-product attention, and that little division by a square root, which looks like a footnote, is the difference between a model that trains and one that doesn't.

Second, softmax. We take those three scaled scores and push them through softmax, which turns any set of numbers into a set of positive weights that sum to one. Now "sat" has, say, seventy percent of its attention on "cat," twenty percent on "the," ten percent on itself — a clean probability distribution over where to look. These are the attention weights.

Third, the payoff. We take those weights and we compute a weighted average of the value vectors. Seventy percent of "cat's" value, twenty percent of "the's" value, ten percent of "sat's" value, added together into a single new vector. That blended vector is what attention hands back for the token "sat." It has literally mixed in information from the tokens it decided were relevant, in the proportions it decided. Do that for every token in parallel and you've completed one attention operation. Query dotted with keys, scaled, softmaxed into weights, weights applied to values. Four steps. Hold that shape in your head, because everything else is a variation on it.

Now, three refinements turn that toy into the thing running in production, and each one has a direct engineering consequence.

Refinement one: causal masking. In a model that generates text, a token must never be allowed to attend to tokens that come after it — when you're predicting word five, word six doesn't exist yet, and if the model could peek at it during training, it would learn to cheat and then fall apart at inference when there's nothing to peek at. So we forbid it. Mechanically, this is delightfully blunt: after computing the score matrix, before the softmax, we take every score that corresponds to a future position and we set it to negative infinity. When negative infinity goes through softmax, it becomes exactly zero weight. The token literally cannot see the future because we've zeroed its ability to. This is called causal masking, and here's why you should care as an engineer. The mask is what lets training be parallel. Because each position can only see the past, the model can process an entire training sequence — predict every next token at every position — in a single forward pass, with the mask guaranteeing that each prediction only used legitimate earlier context. Without the mask you'd have to train one position at a time. The triangular mask is the quiet reason you can train a transformer on a trillion tokens at all.

Refinement two, and this is the big one: multiple heads. Everything I've described so far is a single attention head — one set of query, key, and value matrices, computing one kind of relevance. But one head can only learn one notion of "relevant." Maybe it learns to track syntax, matching verbs to their subjects. But you also want a head tracking long-range topic, and one tracking which pronoun refers to which noun, and one tracking positional patterns. So real models run many attention heads in parallel — a large model might have sixty-four of them per layer. Each head gets its own smaller query, key, and value matrices and operates in its own slice of the vector space, computing its own scores, its own softmax, its own weighted blend of values, completely independently of the others. It's genuinely parallel — the heads don't talk during the operation. After all heads finish, their output vectors are concatenated back together and passed through one final output matrix that mixes them into a single result. This is multi-head attention, and the mental model is: sixty-four independent lenses, each looking for a different kind of relationship, all reading the same sequence at once, and then their findings get merged. The engineering consequence is that attention's parameter and compute cost scales with the number of heads, and — as we'll see in one second — the memory cost of serving does too, which is exactly the pressure that produced the modern variants.

Refinement three: the cost, and here's where this episode connects to the entire rest of your inference life. Look again at the core operation. Every token computes a score against every other token. If you have a thousand tokens, that's a thousand times a thousand — a million scores. Ten thousand tokens, a hundred million scores. The score matrix is square in the sequence length. This is what people mean when they say attention is quadratic. Double your context, quadruple the attention compute. This single fact is the deep reason long context is expensive, and notice it's a different cost from the one we discussed last episode. Last time, the KV cache was a memory problem that grows linearly with sequence length. This — the score matrix — is a compute problem that grows quadratically. Long context hits you twice, in two different currencies, and knowing which is which is the difference between guessing and diagnosing when you're staring at a latency graph.

I'll flag one thing here and leave it for a later episode, because it's worth knowing the escape hatch exists. That quadratic score matrix sounds like it must be built in full, all million or hundred-million entries sitting in memory at once. For years it was, and that materialized matrix was a second, brutal memory cost on top of the compute. The algorithm called flash attention broke that assumption by computing attention in tiles, streaming through the sequence and never storing the full score matrix at all, keeping running totals instead. It doesn't change the quadratic compute — you still do all the multiply-adds — but it removes the quadratic memory of the intermediate matrix and, as a bonus, runs far faster by staying in the fast part of the chip. We'll take that one apart properly when we get to the hardware track. For now, just file it: the compute is unavoidable, but the memory blowup of the score matrix was an implementation detail, and someone fixed it.

Which brings us to the variants you actually see on model cards, and now you have exactly the machinery to understand them.

Go back to the KV cache from last episode. Remember, to avoid recomputing the past on every generated token, we cache the key and value vectors for every token we've already processed. Now count the cost precisely. In plain multi-head attention, every one of those sixty-four heads has its own keys and its own values, so you're caching sixty-four separate sets of key and value vectors for every token in the context. For long contexts and big batches, that cache becomes the single largest consumer of GPU memory during serving — bigger, often, than the model weights themselves. It's the bottleneck.

So here's the move, and it's beautiful in its stinginess. What if the heads shared? The insight is that the queries are where most of the useful diversity lives — you want sixty-four different questions being asked — but the keys and values can be shared across groups of heads without hurting quality much. This is grouped-query attention. You keep all sixty-four query heads, so the model still asks sixty-four different questions, but you have only, say, eight sets of keys and values, each shared by a group of eight query heads. The result: the KV cache shrinks by a factor of eight, because you're storing eight sets of keys and values per token instead of sixty-four. That's exactly the configuration Llama three's seventy-billion-parameter model uses — sixty-four query heads, eight key-value heads, an eight-times reduction in cache size — and it's now the default in most modern open models. Push it to the extreme, where all query heads share a single set of keys and values, and you get multi-query attention, maximum memory savings, a bit more quality loss. Grouped-query is the sweet spot the industry settled on: nearly the memory of the aggressive version, nearly the quality of the full version.

Feel what just happened there. Grouped-query attention isn't some deep new idea. It's a direct, almost mechanical response to the KV cache being the bottleneck — the exact bottleneck we identified last episode. Once you know keys and values are what you cache, and that heads are what multiply the cache, sharing keys and values across heads is the obvious lever. That's the whole point of this track: when you understand the mechanism, the engineering decisions stop being magic incantations from a model card and start being obvious moves given the constraints.

Let me land this with the three takeaways worth keeping.

One. Attention is the only place tokens interact, and its mechanism is four steps you can now recite: each token projects into a query, a key, and a value; every query is scored against every key by a dot product; the scores are scaled and softmaxed into weights; and those weights blend the values into the token's new vector. Everything else in the transformer treats tokens independently. If you remember one shape, remember query-key-value.

Two. Attention costs you in two separate currencies, and they are not the same currency. The score matrix is quadratic in sequence length — that's compute, and it's why doubling context quadruples the attention work. The KV cache is linear in sequence length but multiplied by the number of key-value heads — that's memory, and it's what grouped-query attention exists to shrink. When long context is slow, ask yourself which one is biting.

Three. The variants on the model card — multi-head, grouped-query, multi-query — are all the same four-step operation with a single dial: how many keys and values do you keep versus how many queries do you ask. More sharing, less memory, slightly less quality. That one dial explains the whole family, and you can now read it fluently.

You now understand the keys and values well enough that the cache will feel less like storage and more like the load-bearing wall of the entire serving stack. See you there.
